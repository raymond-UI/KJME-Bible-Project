# Worked example: Luke 22:31–34

One batch traced through every stage. The texts are illustrative. Ids and spans are abbreviated.

## Stage 0–2. Ingest, pretag, batch

**Batch:** `LUK.22.b07`, verses 31 to 34. It is one paragraph, 4 verses, within the 2-to-6 limit.

### KJV text

> **31** And the Lord said, Simon, Simon, behold, Satan hath desired to have you, that he may sift you as wheat:
> **32** But I have prayed for thee, that thy faith fail not: and when thou art converted, strengthen thy brethren.
> **33** And he said unto him, Lord, I am ready to go with thee, both into prison, and to death.
> **34** And he said, I tell thee, Peter, the cock shall not crow this day, before that thou shalt thrice deny that thou knowest me.

### Mechanical draft (`M`)

> **31** … Satan **has** desired to have you⁽ᵖˡ⁾, that he may sift you⁽ᵖˡ⁾ as wheat:
> **32** But I have prayed for you, that your faith fail not: and when you **are** converted, strengthen your brethren.
> **33** … unto him, Lord, I am ready to go with you, …
> **34** … I tell you, Peter, the cock shall not crow this day, before that you **shall** thrice deny that you **know** me.

Note what code got wrong, deliberately: "you are converted". The mechanical draft is correct by its tables, but it reads as religious conversion, a shifted meaning. That is the agents' job.

## Stage 3. Translate

## Stage 5. Review (round 1)

### Labels

- **Scorer:** A = cadence, B = minimalist, C = clarity.
- **Auditor:** A = clarity, B = cadence, C = minimalist.

### Meaning auditor

- **clarity fails G1** on two counts:
  - "asked" adds a request that "desired" does not state. That is a P5 retranslation.
  - "will not fail" turns a subjunctive purpose clause into a prediction (R5).
- **M fails G1:** "are converted" keeps a shifted-meaning word (R4).
- **minimalist** and **cadence** pass.

### Scorer

| cadence | pass | 4 | 5 | 5 | 5 | 0.92 |
| minimalist | pass | 5 | 4 | 4 | 4 | 0.88 |
| clarity | G1 fail ("will not fail") | | | | | ineligible |

The scorer misses "asked" but catches the mood change. Different reviewers catch different things; that is the point of having two.

## Stage 6. Debate

The reviewers agree on the top candidate (cadence), but they disagree on one gate: the auditor fails clarity on "asked", which the scorer missed. The scorer's top two are also only 0.04 apart, under the 0.05 threshold. Either is enough to trigger debate, so one round runs.

- **Scorer, round 2:** accepts the auditor's "asked" finding. Holds its ranking: cadence's "may not fail" is a required syntax change under R5. Minimalist's "fail not" is also defensible, but "unto" goes against a provisional glossary entry.
- **Auditor, round 2:** no change. Both pass.

## What this batch teaches

- **The mechanical layer** handled 12 of the 19 text changes (every pronoun and verb form) with zero model calls, and all the number marking.
- **The auditor** earned its place. The clarity translator's most readable line was the one that changed the meaning.
- **One new syntax rule** came out of one batch. Across a book, the glossary is where the real knowledge piles up.
